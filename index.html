About

I am a postdoctoral scholar in the Steinruecken lab in the department of Ecology and Evolution at the University of Chicago. My current research focuses on the estimation of selective advantage for polygenic traits and monogenic traits amid population structure in ancient humans.

I received my Ph.D. in Statistics and Operations Research from the University of North Carolina in April 2025, and my undergraduate degree in Statistics with a minor in Mathematics from the University of Washington in March 2020. I've had a life-long love for statistics and mathematics. My goal is to continue publishing compelling research for as long as I am able and pass my enthusiasm for statistics to the next generation of researchers!

Research

My current work can be split into two related projects. The first project constitutes a new application for the theme of spatial statistics which ran throughout my Ph.D. work. I aim to create the first method for estimating the selective advantage of genetic variance at a single locus which allows for the inclusion of population structures with migration between subpopulations. I am emphasizing both accuracy and computational feasibility in these methods, as we find novel ways to extend the methods of the single population setting to our more flexible regime. Concurrently, I am working on a method which strives to infer selective advantage in polygenic traits. Though the dynamics of polygenic traits are complex, I aim to create a method which can accurately infer selective advantage while accounting for both directional and stabilizing selection. For both of these projects, I aim to apply the method to time series data of ancient human genomes to identify novel loci/traits which have been under selective pressure.

The primary research of my Ph.D. was in extreme value theory, the study of events in the tails of distributions and order statistics. Spurred by a passion for the outdoors, my work focused on theoretical analysis of multivariate spatial applications of EVT, such as models used to describe extreme heat waves or rainfall events. More specifically, my work has focused on quantifying the bias imposed by the block maxima method, a common sampling scheme in EVT, and developing methods to efficiently estimate the dependence parameters at multiple sites in a spatial region. With my Ph.D. work as a base, I plan to return to EVT, both in the capacity of developing novel statistical theory, and applying cutting-edge techniques to real-world environmental applications.
