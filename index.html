About

I develop statistical methods for data with strong dependence in space and time, with an emphasis on inference that is both theoretically grounded and computationally scalable. I am currently a postdoctoral scholar in the Steinruecken lab in the department of Ecology and Evolution at the University of Chicago, where I apply these tools to time series of ancient human genomes.

I received my Ph.D. in Statistics and Operations Research from the University of North Carolina in April 2025, and my undergraduate degree in Statistics with a minor in Mathematics from the University of Washington in March 2020. I've had a life-long love for statistics and mathematics. My goal is to continue publishing compelling research for as long as I am able and pass my enthusiasm for statistics to the next generation of researchers!

Skills: R, Python (NumPy, Pandas), MATLAB · MCMC, composite likelihood

Research

Dependent time series in structured populations

Estimating selection from allele-frequency time series across subpopulations linked by migration, with a focus on scalable computation.

Natural selection leaves a signature in how allele frequencies change over time, and ancient DNA provides direct time series of those frequencies. Methods for estimating selective advantage are largely built for a single, well-mixed population. I aim to develop the first method for estimating selection at a single locus that allows for population structure with migration between subpopulations (in effect, a spatially structured time series), while keeping inference both accurate and computationally feasible as the model grows.

In parallel, I am developing a method for inferring selection on polygenic traits that accounts for both directional and stabilizing selection. For both projects, the goal is to apply the methods to time series of ancient human genomes to identify loci and traits that have been under selective pressure.

Spatial extremes

Asymptotic theory for block-maxima estimation of the Hüsler-Reiss model, with applications to heat waves and extreme rainfall.

Extreme value theory models the tails of distributions: the heat waves and rainfall events that drive environmental risk. The Hüsler-Reiss distribution is a standard model for the dependence among extremes observed at multiple sites, and it is commonly fit with the block maxima method, which keeps only the largest observation in each block (for example, each year). My work quantifies the bias this sampling scheme introduces and develops theory for efficiently estimating the dependence parameters across a spatial region.

H. Flury, J. Hannig, R. Smith. Extremes, 2026.

Inference for Gaussian processes

Fiducial MCMC for time series and spatial covariance models, with composite likelihood for scalability.

Many time series and spatial models are Gaussian, with all of the structure living in the covariance matrix. Fiducial inference yields a posterior-like distribution without requiring a prior. We developed a fiducial MCMC method that handles non-independent, non-identically distributed data by using the Cayley transform to parameterize the covariance decomposition; the user only needs to supply the covariance function and its derivatives. We demonstrate it on MA(1) and Matérn models, and a composite-likelihood variant cuts runtime from over three hours to about three minutes with comparable estimation quality.

H. Flury, J. Hannig, R. Smith. Journal of Time Series Analysis, 2026.
